# Comparative pilot protocol

## Three independent levels of claim

A coherent mechanism supports a hypothesis. Matched reproducible measurements can support a bounded implementation advantage. A product recommendation additionally needs installation, UX, maintenance, support and actual useful operation. Do not promote one level into the next silently.

Use result labels: DEMONSTRATED_ADVANTAGE, DEMONSTRATED_NONINFERIORITY, DEMONSTRATED_DISADVANTAGE, DOCUMENTED_CAPABILITY, HYPOTHESIS, UNKNOWN and NOT_APPLICABLE. A product can outperform a baseline over a finite declared metric set. No pilot certifies superiority in every imaginable way.

## Baseline selection

Include the simplest competent existing workflow, direct use of a mature library/SDK, the exact fork base where relevant and current upstream. Distinguish competitors from dependencies, inspiration and adjacent products. Expand each material family's research to a genuinely relevant longlist (target at least 25 where the category supports it), but run a justified smaller executable shortlist. Document search exclusions and gaps rather than padding counts or claiming a universal maximum.

The best practical baseline can be a composition of tools. It must be configured competently, not deliberately weakened. Do not charge the owned system for no setup while timing a competitor's entire installation. Keep preprocessing, warm caches and setup costs visible.

## Experimental design

Predeclare the user job, tasks, datasets, invariants, primary/secondary metrics, non-inferiority margins, minimum meaningful effect and stopping rules. Record software versions, hardware, power/thermal settings, runtime limits, credentials scope, network conditions and concurrent workloads. The build used in the experiment must be the actual candidate, not a conveniently different profile.

Use controlled replay to measure framework/runtime overhead; matched live tasks to evaluate performance under the same models/providers/budgets; and separate idiomatic whole-stack implementations to compare practical products. Different language runtimes and implementation styles confound framework attribution; report them explicitly and include relevant same-language baselines.

## Repetitions and uncertainty

Calibrate variance before promising a sample size. Use paired tasks, randomized/counterbalanced execution order and fresh/experienced implementer crossover where possible. Report sample counts, distributions, confidence intervals and failure fractions. Correct interpretation for repeated metrics/multiple comparisons; do not cherry-pick a single favorable p-value. A small inconclusive pilot is a useful result, not proof of parity.

Retain timeouts, malformed results, operator interventions, infrastructure errors and unsuccessful trials. Separate task quality, safety, model/provider failure and environment failure. Re-running until green without reporting earlier failures is unacceptable. Human scoring should be blinded where practical and disagreements adjudicated.

## Writing and maintenance ergonomics

Measure concepts learned, time to first correct result, undocumented behavior, custom glue, error-message quality, type safety, debugger visibility, test construction and change amplification. LOC is supporting context, not the ergonomics score. After implementation, introduce new typed tools, permissions, persistence, provider changes, cancellation, embedding and an upstream upgrade. A framework that is pleasant in a demo but expensive to change may lose.

## Acceptance

Critical invariants are hard gates. Use explicit margins on important dimensions and at least one material advantage/distinct job or a legitimate internal/fork justification. Keep trade-offs visible; do not combine safety and throughput into a flattering weighted score. Negative results can recommend adoption/wrapping of an existing tool, retiring bespoke code, narrowing a promise, or retaining the owned product only for a defensible niche. Such a result completes the experiment, not necessarily the product.
